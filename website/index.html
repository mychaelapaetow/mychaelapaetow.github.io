Market Power and the Supply of Recyclable Waste: Evidence from Indonesia’s Waste Banks

Abstract

Informal recycling markets are ubiquitous in developing countries. Low state capacity for waste management creates opportunities for intermediaries to buy and sell sorted recyclable waste for profit. This paper asks how such intermediaries exercise market power over their sellers and what that implies for the recovery of recyclable material. Using 757,559 transactions from a national network of Indonesian waste banks, we estimate the supply of plastic waste facing a single buyer separately for two types of sellers: (i) households and (ii) waste pickers. We employ a shift-share IV strategy, where identification comes from interacting the price of virgin plastic – a substitute good for plastic waste – with each bank’s predetermined plastic revenue share. We estimate a supply elasticity for pickers at 1.5; households’ is indistinguishable from zero. We then ask how banks respond when competition for these sellers changes, using the staggered opening of rival banks. When a rival bank opens, incumbents lose about a third of their plastic, all of it from pickers. They respond by adding finer, premium grades that only pickers select into, while cutting prices on the broad, mixed categories into which household material falls. Competition thus raises pay for sellers who can leave and widens the markdown on those who cannot. This has important implications for environmental policy. Because households do not respond to price, most of a per-kilogram subsidy would be a transfer to sellers who would have delivered anyway. A payment targeted at pickers would recover an additional ton at about half the cost.

Transboundary Pollution and Political Attribution

with Seonmin Will Heo (UCSB)

Abstract

This paper studies how transboundary pollution enters domestic politics. When air pollution crosses borders, citizens may observe local pollution without knowing whether the source is foreign or domestic, creating scope for politicians either to provide information or to shift blame abroad. We study South Korea, where fine-dust episodes are salient and pollution from China is a recurring focus of public debate. Combining source-specific pollution shocks with a politician-day panel of Facebook posts by South Korean legislators, we distinguish between environmental attention and explicit attribution to China. Instrumental-variables estimates provide little evidence that exogenous increases in local PM2.5 raise politicians’ broad environmental communication. By contrast, the clearest evidence consistent with strategic foreign attribution appears in partisan heterogeneity: conditional on discussing fine dust, conservative politicians increase foreign attribution in response to both transboundary and domestic shocks, whereas Democratic estimates are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. Transboundary environmental problems thus affect politics through selective blame shifting rather than a uniform increase in foreign attribution.

Internalizing Environmental Externalities: A Coasean Approach to Urban Solid Waste Management in Kampala, Uganda

with Aaron Berman (MIT)

Pilot concluded

Funding: IGC

Abstract

Cities in developing countries are generating increasing volumes of solid waste, straining existing waste management systems. Open dumping and burning are common means of disposal, generating meaningful environmental and health externalities, especially in low-income communities and informal settlements. We partner with the Kampala Capital City Authority to test a new intervention designed to deter dumping: installing nets to catch household solid waste that would otherwise flow through rainwater drainage channels, causing flooding downstream. We are piloting a mechanism to elicit downhill households’ willingness-to-pay, and uphill households’ willingness-to-accept, to install nets along shared drainage channels, facilitating Coasean bargaining to realign private and social costs from improper waste management practices.

Spatial Misallocation and Supply-Demand Feedback: Evidence from the Boda-Boda Market in Kampala, Uganda

with Juliana Pinillos (USC) and Santiago De Martini (USC)

Data use agreement signed; pilot concluded

Funding: CEGA

Abstract

We study spatial misallocation in the context of urban mototaxi markets in Kampala, Uganda. Using high-resolution trip data from SafeBoda, Uganda’s leading ride-hailing platform, we document that drivers cluster in a small set of locations while many neighborhoods remain underserved—not because latent demand is low, but because low driver presence discourages ride requests, which in turn discourages drivers from exploring new areas. We argue this feedback loop traps the market in an inefficient equilibrium driven by imperfect information and high search costs on both sides. To test this, we implement a field experiment that provides drivers with real-time information about high-demand areas while notifying riders of nearby driver availability. We expect that coordinated interventions targeting both sides of the market can break the feedback loop and generate welfare gains exceeding those of one-sided approaches, with implications for platform-mediated labor markets in developing economies.

Private Markets for Public Goods: Understanding the Urban Spatial Distribution of Recycling Markets in India

with Vanshika Fotedar (USC)

Funding: IHS

Abstract

Can private markets deliver public goods in the presence of limited state capacity? We study this question in the context of private plastic recycling markets in Maharashtra, India. Informal recycling markets—in which households sell segregated plastic to local middlemen and aggregators—have grown substantially, yet little is known about where these markets emerge or how they interact with the public waste system. We combine ward-level data from the Swachh Survekshan Survey (2024) with a mapping of private plastic recycling operators across Maharashtra and an original survey of household-level plastic prices. Using a spatial model of household recycling decisions, we ask whether private recycling markets substitute for or complement state capacity, and estimate how access to private recyclers affects the flow of plastic waste to municipal landfills.

Safety in Numbers: Collective Risk and the Adoption of Labor-Saving Technology

with Patricia Caetano (USC)

Abstract

Labor-saving technologies often substitute for household tasks that are both time-intensive and dangerous. When such tasks are performed collectively for protection, one household’s adoption makes the remaining group smaller and less safe, so adoption carries a negative externality and may exhibit multiple equilibria. We study this mechanism in Kyaka II, a refugee settlement in Uganda, where women and girls collect firewood in groups along routes with documented exposure to harassment and sexual violence. We partner with an NGO to provide women with briquettes, which eliminate the need for firewood collection. We randomize briquette subsidies to individual households and to entire collection groups identified at baseline. We first test for the externality by comparing take-up under individual and group-level offers and by measuring harassment exposure among untreated members of partially treated groups. We then estimate the effects of eliminating a dangerous home-production activity on women’s and girls’ time use, school attendance, safety, and mental health, using stove-use monitors to measure fuel displacement directly. The results inform why individually targeted subsidies for labor-saving technologies achieve low take-up and how energy-access programs should be designed where electric cooking is not yet feasible.

Fertility at the End of the World: Existential Threat and the Decision to Have Children

Abstract

A growing share of young adults report that fear of climate change, and increasingly of advanced artificial intelligence, shapes their decision on whether to have children. Whether such stated concerns translate into behavior is unknown, and theory does not pin down the sign. A lower expected quality of life for one’s descendants reduces the value of a child, while a compressed personal horizon lowers the opportunity cost of having one now. This project uses the Cold War, when a large majority of Americans believed a nuclear war was plausible within their lifetimes, as a historical laboratory for measuring how civilization-scale threats affect fertility. I exploit the November 20, 1983 broadcast of The Day After, a dramatization of nuclear war watched by roughly 100 million Americans that produced a documented, sharp rise in fear of nuclear conflict. Combining complete-count daily birth records for every U.S. county with market-level variation in the broadcast’s audience, I estimate the response of conceptions in the weeks following the broadcast. Because the nuclear threat generated subjective probabilities of catastrophe far larger than any climate or AI survey has recorded, these estimates bound what contemporary existential anxieties can plausibly do to fertility and to other long-horizon investments. A companion survey experiment elicits subjective expectations about climate, AI, and nuclear risk and randomizes information that shifts them, to identify which mechanism—concern for descendants, a shortened horizon, or the ethics of adding a person to the world—drives the modern decision.
