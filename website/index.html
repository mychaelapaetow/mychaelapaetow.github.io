About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University of Southern California. My research is in environmental and development economics, with a focus on waste, recycling, and pollution in low- and middle-income countries. I study how markets and institutions—often informal ones—shape who bears the cost of environmental externalities, combining field experiments with novel administrative and satellite data in Indonesia, Uganda, India, and South Korea.

I will be available for interviews during the 2026–2027 academic job market.

Market Power and the Supply of Recyclable Waste: Evidence from Indonesia’s Waste Banks

with Aaron Berman (MIT)

Abstract

Informal recycling markets divert large volumes of plastic from dumps and waterways in developing countries, yet the intermediaries that buy recyclables often face little local competition. We study market power in Indonesia’s network of waste banks (bank sampah)—community buyers that purchase sorted recyclables at posted prices—using novel transaction-level microdata. First, using a shift-share instrument that interacts the price of virgin plastic with each bank’s exposure to plastic, we estimate the supply elasticity of recyclable plastic and show that banks pass through upstream price shocks primarily by adding finer grades to their menus rather than repricing existing ones. Second, we document sharp heterogeneity across sellers: informal waste pickers are highly price-elastic, while households are not. Third, exploiting the staggered entry of rival banks, we show that incumbents lose plastic volume and picker sellers when a competitor opens nearby, and that household-supplied incumbents respond by cutting prices to their captive base. Monopsony power under uniform pricing therefore compounds the environmental externality, leaving plastic recovery below its efficient level.

Internalizing Environmental Externalities: A Coasean Approach to Urban Solid Waste Management in Kampala, Uganda

with Aaron Berman (MIT)

Pilot concluded; scale-up underway

Abstract

Cities in developing countries are generating increasing volumes of solid waste, straining existing waste management systems. Open dumping and burning are common means of disposal, generating meaningful environmental and health externalities, especially in low-income communities and informal settlements. We partner with the Kampala Capital City Authority to test a new intervention designed to deter dumping: installing nets to catch household solid waste that would otherwise flow through rainwater drainage channels, causing flooding downstream. We are piloting a mechanism to elicit downhill households’ willingness-to-pay, and uphill households’ willingness-to-accept, to install nets along shared drainage channels, facilitating Coasean bargaining to realign private and social costs from improper waste management practices.

Environmental Scapegoating and the Political Economy Implications of Transboundary Pollution

with Seonmin Will Heo (UCSB)

Draft available upon request

Abstract

Transboundary pollution is an increasingly common phenomenon that raises interesting and complex questions about environmental accountability and cooperation. In this paper, we study the political economy impact of transboundary PM2.5. We obtain data on hourly particle trajectories to estimate how transboundary PM2.5 affects various political outcomes, including environmental legislation, regulatory behavior, and rhetoric. We introduce a novel mechanism—environmental scapegoating—to help explain the behavior of politicians faced with significant transboundary pollution inflows.

Spatial Misallocation and Supply-Demand Feedback: Evidence from the Boda-Boda Market in Kampala, Uganda

with Juliana Pinillos (USC) and Santiago De Martini (USC)

Data use agreement signed; pilot underway

Abstract

We study spatial misallocation in the context of urban mototaxi markets in Kampala, Uganda. Using high-resolution trip data from SafeBoda, Uganda’s leading ride-hailing platform, we document that drivers cluster in a small set of locations while many neighborhoods remain underserved—not because latent demand is low, but because low driver presence discourages ride requests, which in turn discourages drivers from exploring new areas. We argue this feedback loop traps the market in an inefficient equilibrium driven by imperfect information and high search costs on both sides. To test this, we implement a field experiment that provides drivers with real-time information about high-demand areas while notifying riders of nearby driver availability. We expect that coordinated interventions targeting both sides of the market can break the feedback loop and generate welfare gains exceeding those of one-sided approaches, with implications for platform-mediated labor markets in developing economies.

Private Markets for Public Goods: Understanding the Urban Spatial Distribution of Recycling Markets in India

with Vanshika Fotedar (USC)

Abstract

Can private markets deliver public goods in the presence of limited state capacity? We study this question in the context of private plastic recycling markets in Maharashtra, India. Informal recycling markets—in which households sell segregated plastic to local middlemen and aggregators—have grown substantially, yet little is known about where these markets emerge or how they interact with the public waste system. We combine ward-level data from the Swachh Survekshan Survey (2024) with a mapping of private plastic recycling operators across Maharashtra and an original survey of household-level plastic prices. Using a spatial model of household recycling decisions, we ask whether private recycling markets substitute for or complement state capacity, and estimate how access to private recyclers affects the flow of plastic waste to municipal landfills.
